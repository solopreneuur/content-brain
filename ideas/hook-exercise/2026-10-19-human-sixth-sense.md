# Human Sixth Sense

Bitter builds method

**On the $1M flip for Humanities Sixth Sense:** in his version, the flip works because the study result (only 2.5% got it) turns into a reversal of who wins. Experts lose because they skip steps; beginners and polymaths win. So your flip needs the same shape: a guessable test, a surprising result, then the person your viewer assumes is ahead actually loses, and the person your viewer is actually wins. If Humanities Sixth Sense can end on "that's why someone like you has the edge," it'll work.

**[HOOK: stakes]** $1 million on the line.

**[CHALLENGE: viewer plays along]** If your friend was tapping the rhythm of Happy Birthday, could you guess correctly that it was Happy Birthday?

**[EXPECTATION: set up the obvious answer]** Seems like a pretty obvious yes, right?

**[RESULT: study + surprising number]** This was actually a Stanford study in 1990, and only 2.5% of people got it right.

**[WHY: explain the gap]** The people tapping could hear the melody in their head, and it seemed so obvious, but the listeners just heard random taps.

**[CONCEPT: name it]** This is known as the curse of knowledge,

**[WHO LOSES: the assumed winner]** and it explains why experts make such bad teachers. Because they skip steps that seem obvious to them but aren't obvious to anyone else. And they're simply unable to see the topic from the perspective of the beginner.

**[FLIP: who actually wins]** Which is why beginners have such a huge advantage that nobody talks about. They have a fresh perspective and the ability to see the broader picture.

**[REFRAME: closing line, no CTA]** Because it's not the expert in one field that's most creative, but the polymath that's acquired the ability to learn and can draw from multiple sources of inspiration
