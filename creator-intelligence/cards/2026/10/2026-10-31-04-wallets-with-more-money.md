# Wallets with more cash come back more

**The find:** Researchers handed in 17,303 "lost" wallets in 355 cities across 40 countries. With no money inside, 40% were reported to the owner; with about $13, 51%; with about $94, 72%.
**The surprise:** Almost everyone, economists included, predicted the opposite: that more cash would mean fewer returns.
**The idea:** [Civic honesty](../../../concepts/civic-honesty.md). People keep the money when it's cheap to see themselves as honest. The bigger the sum, the more keeping it feels like theft, so honesty goes up with the stakes.

## In depth

Alain Cohn, Michel Maréchal, David Tannenbaum and Christian Zünd ran the experiment between 2013 and 2016 and published it in *Science* in June 2019. Research assistants walked into banks, hotels, post offices, police stations, museums and theatres in 355 cities across 40 countries. Each handed a receptionist a transparent business-card case and said: "Hi, I found this on the street just around the corner. Somebody must have lost it. I'm in a hurry and have to go. Can you please take care of it?" Then they left.

Each wallet held three identical business cards with an email address, a shopping list and a key. Some held no money; most held the equivalent of $13.45 in local currency. In the US, the UK and Poland, a third version held $94.15. The researchers counted how many recipients emailed the "owner".

In 38 of 40 countries, wallets with money were returned more often than empty ones. Globally, 40% of empty wallets were reported, against 51% with the small sum. In the three big-money countries, the return rate rose to 72%. Switzerland and Norway were near the top; rates were lowest in China and Morocco, but even there money raised returns.

Two details point to why. Adding a key, worth nothing to the finder but useful to the owner, raised reporting by itself, which suggests people care about the owner. And when the team asked people to imagine keeping the wallet, they said keeping a bigger sum felt more like stealing. The authors' reading: people want to keep a self-image as honest, and that gets harder the more money is involved.

Then they asked a representative sample of Americans, and 279 economists, to predict the results. Both groups got it wrong, expecting fewer returns as the money went up.
