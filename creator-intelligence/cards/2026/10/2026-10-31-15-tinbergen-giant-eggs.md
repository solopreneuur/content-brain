# Birds chose giant fake eggs

**The find:** Nobel-winning biologist Niko Tinbergen made plaster eggs bigger and more vividly marked than real ones. Birds abandoned their own eggs to sit on the fakes, even ones so big they kept sliding off.
**The surprise:** An exaggerated fake beat the real thing, even when it plainly didn't work.
**The idea:** A [supernormal stimulus](../../../concepts/supernormal-stimulus.md): an exaggerated version of whatever an instinct looks for. Psychologist Deirdre Barrett argues junk food, beauty filters and clickbait use the same trick on people.

## Deeper · 2026-10-31

Niko Tinbergen built his career on taking instinct apart piece by piece. Working with herring gulls on the Dutch coast and later at Oxford, he and his students didn't just watch birds, they built cardboard and plaster props to find the exact feature an animal's brain was keying on. Newly hatched herring gull chicks peck at a red spot on the tip of their parent's yellow bill, which triggers the parent to regurgitate food. Tinbergen and his student A.C. Perdeck ran the chicks through a gauntlet of models: a real stuffed gull head, a painted wooden head, a flat cardboard cutout, a bare bill with no head at all. The cutout worked almost as well as the real head. Then they tried a thin red stick with a few white bands painted on, nothing like a gull at all, and the chicks pecked at it harder and faster than at any lifelike model. The chicks weren't recognizing "parent." They were responding to a narrow trigger, a red mark against a contrasting background, and a crude exaggeration of that one feature beat the real bird.

Tinbergen called the rule a sign stimulus and the automatic response it triggers an innate releasing mechanism. An exaggerated version of the sign stimulus, one with more contrast, more saturation or more size than anything in nature, is a supernormal stimulus, and it fires the mechanism harder than the real cue it evolved to detect. The egg experiments are the cleanest version. Species that lay small, dull, speckled eggs were given plaster eggs painted a louder, more saturated version of their own pattern and built oversized. Birds left their own eggs to incubate the fakes, in some cases eggs so big they slid off the nest and the bird climbed back on anyway. Konrad Lorenz, who shared the 1973 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Tinbergen and Karl von Frisch for founding ethology, independently noted the same thing: given a choice, brooding birds picked the biggest egg on offer over their own.

The pattern wasn't limited to eggs and beaks. Tinbergen kept male three-spined sticklebacks, which flush red on the belly in breeding season and attack any rival male that enters their territory, in tanks near a lab window. His students noticed the fish repeatedly charging the glass on the side facing the street. The trigger was a red post-office van passing outside. Tinbergen wrote that "even a red mail van passing our windows at a distance of 100 yards could make the males in the tank charge its glass side in that direction." He confirmed it with models: a crude blob painted red underneath drew more attacks than a perfect, colorless replica of a stickleback. Decades later, in 1983, entomologists Darryl Gwynne and David Rentz documented Australian jewel beetles trying to mate with discarded brown stubby beer bottles, whose glossy, dimpled surface out-signaled real females on size and texture; the finding won the 2011 Ig Nobel Prize in Biology.

Harvard Medical School psychologist Deirdre Barrett carried the idea to humans in two books, *Waistland* (2007) and *Supernormal Stimuli: How Primal Urges Overran Their Evolutionary Purpose* (2010). Her argument is evolutionary mismatch: instincts tuned on the savanna for scarce salt, sugar and fat, for faces and motion, for status and territory, now meet a built environment engineered to max out those same triggers. Processed food concentrates sweetness and fat past anything that existed before agriculture; pornography and television present idealized faces and exaggerated social cues at a volume no ancestral environment could; nationalism, she argued, hijacks territorial defense instincts into war. The *Wall Street Journal*'s review called the book timely but faulted Barrett for weighing the costs of supernormal stimuli without weighing their benefits.

The extension to humans is also where the theory gets contested. Critics, notably Bart du Laing and Andreas de Block in a 2010 *Philosophical Psychology* paper, point out that the ethological machinery underneath the term, the innate releasing mechanism and the fixed action pattern, has been largely abandoned by working ethologists since Tinbergen's day because real animal behavior turned out to be far less rigid and hardwired than those models assumed. They also note that humans overeating sugary, fatty food may be a story about appetite-regulation systems malfunctioning, not simply a bigger, redder spot winning a reflex. The bird experiments themselves are not in question. Whether beauty filters and junk food work on people the same mechanical way a red stick works on a gull chick is the part nobody has nailed down.
