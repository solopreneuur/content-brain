# Japan's family firms adopt grown men

**The find:** About 98% of adoptions in Japan are of men in their mid-20s to 30s, not children. Family companies adopt a talented manager, often by marrying him to a daughter, and hand him the firm. Suzuki and Panasonic were both run by adopted sons-in-law.
**The surprise:** Studying listed Japanese firms over half a century, economists found that companies run by adopted heirs outperformed companies run by blood heirs.
**The idea:** [Mukoyōshi](../../../concepts/mukoyoshi.md). Widen the pool you pick successors from, and the blood heirs work harder too, because they know they can be replaced.

## In depth

In most countries, a family business has a simple succession problem: the founder's children may not be any good, and the firm goes down with them. Japan solved this centuries ago with *mukoyōshi*, the adopted son-in-law. A family with a capable young manager but no suitable son (or an unsuitable one) marries him to a daughter, legally adopts him, and he takes the family name and the top job. Adult adoption is so normal that, by one widely cited figure, about 98% of Japanese adoptions are of men aged roughly 25 to 30.

The practice runs through famous companies. Osamu Suzuki, who led Suzuki Motor for decades, was born Osamu Matsuda and married into the Suzuki family. Konosuke Matsushita, Panasonic's founder, handed the presidency to Masaharu Matsushita, born Masaharu Hirata, his adopted son-in-law. Kongō Gumi, the temple builder that ran from 578 AD until 2006, used the same device to keep the line going.

In 2011, Vikas Mehrotra, Randall Morck, Jungwook Shim and Yupana Wiwattanakantang tested whether it works. Using nearly every listed non-financial Japanese firm from the postwar decades, they compared firms run by blood heirs, adopted heirs and professional managers. Firms run by adopted heirs outperformed those run by blood heirs and roughly matched founder-run firms. About 10% of successions in their data went to a son-in-law, adopted son or adopted son-in-law.

They proposed two mechanisms. First, adoption cuts off the bottom of the talent distribution: the least able sons simply don't get the job. Second, the threat changes behaviour. A Japanese blood son knows a sharper outsider can be brought in over his head, so he has a reason to invest in his own skills. In other countries, a founder's son can take the job for granted.

The finding is contested. A later Berkeley analysis argued the performance differences are not statistically significant. But the practice itself is not in dispute, and it turns a dynasty into something closer to a tournament.
