# Costco caps its own markup at 14%

**The find:** Costco co-founder Jim Sinegal set a rule that no branded item is marked up more than 14%, and no own-label item more than 15%, even when shoppers would happily pay more. A Deutsche Bank analyst complained that Costco was better at serving members and employees than shareholders.
**The surprise:** Wall Street called the cap money left on the table. Costco has kept it for decades, and its stock has far outrun the market.
**The idea:** [Self-imposed constraint](../../../concepts/self-imposed-constraint.md). Sinegal warned that going to 16% or 18% would start a slide; a hard rule protects discipline, and customers' trust, better than case-by-case judgement.

## Deeper · 2026-10-31

Jim Sinegal and Jeffrey Brotman opened the first Costco in Seattle on September 15, 1983, building on the cost-plus model Sinegal had learned as an employee of Sol Price at FedMart and Price Club. The rule he set, and never changed, is mechanical: no branded item can be marked up more than 14% over what Costco paid for it, no Kirkland Signature private-label item more than 15%, and electronics closer to 6-8%. It isn't a guideline buyers can push against. Pricing is calculated automatically from supplier cost; if a buyer negotiates a lower cost, the retail price drops with it, because the system has no field letting them bank the difference as margin. The result is a company-wide gross margin around 11-12%, against 25% at Walmart, 25-50% at a typical supermarket, and 50-300% at department and convenience stores.

The rule got its most public test in 2005, when the New York Times' Steven Greenhouse profiled Costco under the headline "How Costco Became the Anti-Wal-Mart." In it, Deutsche Bank retail analyst Bill Dreher said flatly that at Costco "it's better to be an employee or a customer than a shareholder" — a direct complaint that Sinegal's generosity to workers and shoppers was money the stock price should have captured. Sinegal's answer, on record in the same piece: "On Wall Street, they're in the business of making money between now and next Thursday... We want to build a company that will still be here 50 and 60 years from now." On the specific temptation to loosen the cap, he invoked Sears: "When I started, Sears, Roebuck was the Costco of the country, but they allowed someone else to come in under them. We don't want to be one of the casualties." Pushed on why not edge the cap to 16% or 18%, where almost no shopper would notice, he warned that was the start of a slide that would eat the whole discipline — a point he'd made a year earlier to Bloomberg BusinessWeek's Michelle Conlin in the quote that's followed him since: "You could raise the price of a bottle of ketchup to $1.03 instead of $1, and no one would know. Raising prices just 3% per product would add 50% to your pretax income. Why not do it? It's like heroin. You do a little and you want a little bit more. Raising prices is the easy way." The same logic shows up in a story about a jeans buyer who found a big discount and wanted to keep part of it as margin: Sinegal made him pass it all to the customer, reportedly telling him taking the extra markup was "like taking heroin. You can't stop."

The cap's most famous application isn't dry goods but the $1.50 hot dog-and-soda combo, on the menu since the mid-1980s. By 2013, successor CEO Craig Jelinek told Sinegal the combo was losing money and he wanted to raise it to $1.75. Sinegal's reply, which Jelinek has repeated in interviews ever since: "If you raise the effing hot dog, I will kill you. Figure it out." Jelinek figured it out by building Kirkland Signature hot-dog plants in Los Angeles and Chicago and switching the fountain drink from Coca-Cola to Pepsi — changing everything around the price rather than the price itself. Current CEO Ron Vachris has made the same commitment publicly, and Costco now sells well over 100 million combos a year at a price that would be roughly $4.50-4.65 today if it had merely tracked inflation.

Costco's 10-K filings formalize the posture in writing, telling shareholders the company does "not focus in the short-term on maximizing prices charged" but instead protects members' trust in its "pricing authority" — language that makes the constraint a disclosed, institutional commitment rather than a personal quirk any future CEO could quietly drop. It's also subsidized by a second business: membership fees, which run close to 100% gross margin and account for roughly 70% of operating income, meaning Costco doesn't need merchandise margin to be profitable the way a typical retailer does. That's the strongest case against treating the 14% cap as a universally exportable lesson — it works partly because membership fees, not markup, carry the profit load. What isn't contested is the output: Costco's stock has compounded at roughly 16-18% annually since its December 1985 IPO, badly outrunning the S&P 500 over the same four decades, through three CEOs who each kept the same rule.
