# Ferrari's pit crew redesigned child surgery

**The find:** Exhausted after a long operation, two heart surgeons at London's Great Ormond Street Hospital watched a Formula 1 pit stop on TV and saw the same handover they had just botched, done better. They went to Ferrari, copied its pit-stop drill for moving a child from theatre to intensive care, and technical errors fell by 42%.
**The surprise:** The fix for a children's hospital came from a racing team, not from medicine.
**The idea:** [Cross-domain transfer](../../../concepts/cross-domain-transfer.md). Your hardest problem has often been solved already, in an industry you'd never think to look at.

## In depth

The most dangerous minutes after children's heart surgery are often not in the operating theatre. They come during the transfer to intensive care, when a tiny patient is moved with a ventilator, monitors, drains and drips, and the theatre team hands information to the ICU team. At Great Ormond Street Hospital in London, cardiac surgeon Martin Elliott and intensive care specialist Allan Goldman knew these handovers were chaotic: several people talking at once, equipment swapped in no fixed order, details lost.

The story they tell is that after a long day in theatre they watched a Formula 1 race on TV and saw a pit stop: around twenty people changing four tyres and refuelling a car in seconds, without a word and without errors. That was a handover too. They contacted Ferrari and McLaren, visited the teams and filmed both the pit stops and their own handovers. Human factors researchers, including Ken Catchpole, broke both down step by step. Aviation training captains were consulted as well.

The lessons that carried over were simple. Every handover got one leader, the anaesthetist, who stood where they could see everything, like the lollipop man in the pit lane. Each person had one defined job. Tasks happened in a fixed order: equipment and monitoring first, then the information handover, then the plan for the next hours. During the technical phase, people talked only when they had to. A checklist sat in the middle.

The results, published in *Paediatric Anaesthesia* in 2007, showed technical errors per handover down 42% and information omissions down 49%. The handover did not get slower. Goldman pointed to the cascade effect: in Formula 1, a stuck fuel hose turns a small error into a lost race, and in the ICU, small handover slips stack the same way. The protocol spread to other hospitals.
