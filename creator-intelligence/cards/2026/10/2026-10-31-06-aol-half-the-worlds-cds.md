# Half the world's CDs said AOL

**The find:** AOL's free sign-up discs cost the company more than $300 million. At the campaign's peak, by its marketing chief's account, half of all CDs manufactured in the world carried an AOL logo, and AOL signed up a new subscriber every six seconds.
**The surprise:** In 1998 the biggest presser of compact discs in America wasn't a record label. It was an internet company giving them away.
**The idea:** The [mere-exposure effect](../../../concepts/mere-exposure-effect.md), run as carpet bombing. When the product is unfamiliar, being everywhere does the persuading.

2 · Shocking number · Marketing · [TechCrunch, quoting AOL's former CMO Jan Brandt](https://techcrunch.com/2010/12/27/aol-discs-90s/)

## In depth

America Online's problem in the early 1990s was that most people had never been online and had no idea what it was for. Jan Brandt, who joined as head of marketing in 1993, reasoned that nobody would understand AOL from an ad; they had to try it. So she put the software in their hands, for free, first on floppy disks, then on CDs, with a free trial of hours online.

The early mailings worked far better than direct mail normally does, so AOL kept scaling. Discs went into magazines, cereal boxes and product packaging, and were stacked at checkouts. Brandt called herself the "carpet bombing queen". The Smithsonian's National Museum of American History, which holds AOL discs in its collection, repeats her claim that at the height of the campaign half of all CDs produced worldwide were AOL discs. She told TechCrunch the programme cost more than $300 million, and that at its peak AOL was adding a subscriber every six seconds. Over its life the campaign put more than a billion discs into circulation.

It worked. AOL grew from a few hundred thousand subscribers in the early 1990s to around 25 million by the early 2000s, and in 2000 it was big enough to buy Time Warner. For millions of Americans, as the new 99% Invisible episode puts it, for a while the internet was AOL.

The discs also became a joke and a nuisance. People turned them into coasters, mobiles and art projects. The 99% Invisible episode, part of its *History of the United States in 100 Objects* series, picks the disc as the object that stands for how America got online, and asks why AOL faded once broadband made the dial-up disc pointless.
