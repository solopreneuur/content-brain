# The shopping cart needed fake shoppers

**The find:** When Sylvan Goldman put the first shopping carts in his Oklahoma City supermarkets in 1937, nobody used them. He hired men and women of different ages to push carts around the store, filling them like ordinary shoppers.
**The surprise:** Men found the carts emasculating and women said they already pushed enough prams. The object everyone now uses without thinking had to be faked into normal.
**The idea:** [Social proof](../../../concepts/social-proof.md). People don't adopt a new thing because it's useful. They adopt it when they see people like them already using it.

## In depth

Sylvan Goldman owned the Humpty Dumpty and Standard Food Markets chains in Oklahoma City. In the mid-1930s he noticed that shoppers stopped buying once their hand baskets got heavy. One night in 1936, looking at two folding wooden chairs in his office, he imagined putting a basket on each seat and wheels on the legs. With a handyman, Fred Young, he built a folding frame that held two wire baskets, one above the other.

The carts launched on 4 June 1937, with newspaper ads promising that customers could shop without carrying a basket. They were ignored. Men, by Goldman's account, felt they looked weak pushing one; they could carry a basket. Women said it looked too much like the baby carriages they had just escaped. Store staff demonstrated them at the entrance and still nobody took one.

So Goldman hired people, men and women, young and old, to walk the aisles pushing carts and filling them as if they were ordinary customers. A greeter at the door offered real shoppers a cart. Once customers saw people like themselves using the carts, they started taking them. Within a few years the carts were standard in his stores, and supermarkets elsewhere wanted them.

Goldman set up the Folding Carrier Basket Company, patented the design and later licensed the nesting cart, the telescoping kind that slides into a line. He collected a royalty on carts across the United States and became a multimillionaire. The cart changed retail economics too: larger loads meant bigger stores, bigger car parks and the out-of-town supermarket.

The launch problem was never engineering. It was the fear of looking odd. Goldman solved it by staging the behaviour he wanted until it stopped looking odd.
