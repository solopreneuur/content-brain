# Thirsty people imagine thirstier hikers

**The find:** Researchers stopped people at a gym, either before or right after a workout, and asked them to imagine hikers lost without food or water. The ones who had just exercised were much more likely to say the hikers would suffer more from thirst than hunger.
**The surprise:** A twenty-minute workout changed how people judged a stranger's suffering in a story.
**The idea:** The [hot-cold empathy gap](../../../concepts/hot-cold-empathy-gap.md). We predict what other people feel (and what we'll feel later) from the state our body is in right now, and we underestimate how much that state is steering us.

9 · Unfamiliar concept · Psychology · [Van Boven & Loewenstein, Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, 2003](https://www.andrew.cmu.edu/user/gl20/GeorgeLoewenstein/Papers_files/pdf/vb_loew_2003.pdf)

## In depth

The hot-cold empathy gap is George Loewenstein's name for a blind spot: when we're calm, fed or comfortable ("cold"), we can't really imagine how hunger, fear, arousal or pain ("hot") will drive us, and the other way round.

In 2003, Leaf Van Boven and Loewenstein tested whether the gap also distorts how we read other people. They approached people at a university gym either just before or just after a cardio workout. Each read a short story about hikers lost in the mountains without food or water, then answered which would bother the hikers more, hunger or thirst, and how they themselves would feel in the same spot.

People who had just exercised, and so were thirsty and warm, were markedly more likely to say thirst would be the hikers' worst problem. The effect ran through the self: the workout changed how participants thought *they* would feel lost in the mountains, and that drove what they assumed the hikers felt. In a companion study, most participants said outright that they judged the hikers by imagining trading places with them.

Later work by the same group added a twist. In a 2012 follow-up, cold or thirsty participants projected their state onto a hiker only when told the hiker shared their politics. A thirsty liberal felt a liberal hiker's thirst, but not a conservative hiker's.

The gap shows up in everyday decisions too. In a well-known study by Daniel Read and Barbara van Leeuwen, office workers choosing a snack for a week later mostly picked fruit when they had just eaten, and mostly picked chocolate when they were hungry. Loewenstein's point is that we don't just feel differently in different states. We predict other people's behaviour, and our own, from whichever state we happen to be in.
