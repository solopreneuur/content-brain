# A loom patent paid for Toyota

**The find:** Toyota began as a loom company. In 1929 Sakichi Toyoda sold the rights to his automatic loom, which stopped itself the moment a thread broke, to Britain's Platt Brothers for £100,000, and passed the money to his son Kiichiro to research cars.
**The surprise:** A British textile-machinery firm, in an industry Toyota would later leave behind, helped bankroll Japan's biggest carmaker.
**The idea:** [Jidoka](../../../concepts/jidoka.md), "automation with a human touch": a machine that stops itself when something goes wrong, so one worker can watch many. The same idea became a core rule of the Toyota Production System.

## Deeper · 2026-10-31

Sakichi Toyoda spent four decades on looms before anyone thought of him as a carmaker. He built his first wooden hand loom in 1890, a wood-and-iron power loom in 1896, and founded Toyoda Automatic Loom Works in 1926 to commercialize his life's work. Its flagship, the Type G, finished in 1924 and put into assembly-line production in 1927, was the world's first loom with non-stop shuttle change: a wood peg riding against the thread on each shuttle's spool dropped into a slot the moment the thread ran out, triggering the machine to eject the spent shuttle and kick in a fresh one without stopping. Layered onto that were Sakichi's older stop-on-break devices: a metal dropper hanging on every warp thread that fell and jammed an oscillating bar if that thread snapped, and a three-pronged fork that could slip through a metal grille only once the weft thread broke, tripping a shutdown. Between the shuttle-change motion and the breakage sensors, one worker could mind 30 to 50 looms instead of one. Taiichi Ohno later pointed to exactly this mechanism, a machine that stops itself and signals a human rather than running on and compounding a defect, as the origin of jidoka, which became one of the Toyota Production System's two pillars alongside just-in-time.

The Type G's reputation reached Britain's Platt Brothers of Oldham, then one of the world's largest textile-machinery makers. On 21 December 1929, Kiichiro Toyoda, Sakichi's son and the engineer who held the loom's basic patent, signed a deal giving Platt Brothers production and sales rights everywhere except Japan, China and the United States, for £100,000 sterling, paid in four annual instalments of £25,000. It did not close cleanly: Platt Brothers complained the technical documentation Toyoda supplied was thin, and demanded a lower price; Toyoda countered that Platt's own manufacturing of the licensed looms was substandard. Combined with the deepening world depression after 1929, the dispute dragged the final payout down to roughly £83,500, not the full £100,000 most retellings quote.

Sakichi was already turning his attention to cars. He told Kiichiro directly, "Build cars, and serve your country," and reportedly argued every person should attempt one major undertaking in a lifetime, that the loom had been his, and the automobile should be his son's. Sakichi died on 30 October 1930, before any of it existed as a company, and by most accounts it was effectively his deathbed wish that the Platt money, industrial capital earned from looms, be redirected into automotive research rather than back into the loom business. Kiichiro, trained at Tokyo Imperial University, had already used a 1929 trip to finalize the Platt contract to detour through the United States and tour Ford's plants. An automotive department opened inside Toyoda Automatic Loom Works on 1 September 1933; its engineers bought and tore down a 1933 Chevrolet, a Ford truck and a 1934 DeSoto to learn how American components fit together before designing their own. The department's prototype factory in Kariya produced the A1 passenger car and G1 truck in 1935, and Toyota Motor Co., Ltd. was incorporated as its own company on 28 August 1937, with the name changed from Toyoda to Toyota for a cleaner sound and an eight-stroke count considered luckier in Japanese numerology. Its first president, installed in 1937, was Rizaburo Toyoda, born Kodama, who had married Sakichi's daughter Aiko and taken the family name, the same mukoyōshi adoption pattern behind today's other featured card on Suzuki and Panasonic, just with a loom-and-car dynasty nobody usually files under that heading.

The part of the story that gets smoothed over in most retellings is who actually owned the thing being sold. A 2010s reconsideration of the Toyoda-Platt agreement by researchers revisiting Toyota's own archives found that the basic Type G patent was registered to Kiichiro, not to Sakichi, which complicates the clean "father funds son" version where an aging inventor single-handedly bankrolls his heir's new industry. The more accurate picture is a family and a company, Toyoda Automatic Loom Works, moving its own capital and its own engineer-turned-patent-holder from textiles into cars together, with Sakichi supplying the conviction and the deathbed instruction rather than a lone cheque. What survives contact with the archives is the mechanism, not just the money: the self-stopping loom is the direct ancestor of jidoka, still one of two words Toyota engineers use to explain how the company builds cars.
